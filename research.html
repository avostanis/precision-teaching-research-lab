01The system

Precision teaching, in plain English

Precision teaching is not a teaching method. It is a way of measuring learning and deciding what to do next, and it works alongside any curriculum or teaching approach. It shapes how we think about teaching, and its charts give precise, frequent feedback on each learner’s progress. Together, these make teaching more precise. Ogden Lindsley founded it in 1965, and teachers developed it in their classrooms. It has five steps. Select each one to see how it works.

More on fluency

How we check for true mastery

Fluent skills are expected to bring certain by-products with them. These still need more research. We use them as practical tests of true mastery: we do not call a skill mastered because the learner reached a target number. We check what they can do with it, and that makes our work more rigorous. More on agility

Learning to learn faster

Fluency is about one skill. Agility is about what happens across skills. A learner shows agility when each new, related skill is learned more quickly than the one before. In short, they are learning how to learn.

Fluency shows in how many correct responses a learner gives per minute. Agility shows in the celeration, the slope of the learning picture, which is why it takes a Standard Celeration Chart to see it. Here a learner practises three times tables, one after another. Select a sign of agility to see it on the chart.

A learner may show one of these signs or both, and there are other ways to measure agility (Meyer et al., 2021). Agility is not the same as application. Application means using a skill inside a more complex one. Agility means learning the next, similar skill faster. Agility has been studied far less than fluency, and how to bring it about reliably is one of the questions we are working on.

Sources: Meyer, Newsome, Fuller, Newsome and Ghezzi (2021); Vostanis (2027). “Celerating celerations” is Lindsley’s (2000) phrase and “climbing bottoms” is Neely’s (2003).

04Our aims

What we are working towards

Aims: draft wording

  1. Strengthen precision teaching

    Test and refine the parts that make the system work: pinpointing, Standard Celeration Charting and decisions based on the data.

  2. Understand fluency and agility

    Study how skills become fluent, how fluent skills support more complex ones, and how learners come to learn faster. Along the way, we aim to discover more principles of performance and learning.

  3. Take precision teaching to new places

    Precision teaching has mostly been used with academic skills. We think it can do much more, so we test it in new areas, including non-academic skills, staff training and settings beyond education.

  4. Strengthen evidence-based practice

    Precision teaching is our main focus, but not our only one. We also study other evidence-based approaches to teaching and support, and how precise measurement can help them work better.

  5. Make research accessible

    Publish openly and share findings in plain English, so that the people who can use them do not need to read a journal first.
